What do you want to understand?

Every lesson is self-contained. Open one, close it, open another.

Feminism is the belief that people of all genders should have equal rights, opportunities and freedom, together with the organised effort to make that real. It studies how societies have handed men more power by default, and it works to change the rules, habits and institutions that keep it that way.

Two things are worth separating. Feminism is a belief about equality, and it is also a movement that does something about it. You can agree with the belief and still be learning the movement's history. Most people start there.

It is about gender as a whole, not only about women. Rigid ideas of masculinity hurt boys too: they are the reason "man up" gets said to a fourteen-year-old who needs help. A feminist analysis has room for that.

What it looks like at school

Two students hand in equally strong physics tests. One is asked whether she is sure she wants to continue with the subject next year; the other is asked which university he is aiming for. Nobody in that room intends any harm. The pattern is still doing work, and it is the kind of pattern feminism exists to notice and interrupt.

Why it matters

Because the effects are measurable, not theoretical. In Canada, women aged 25 to 54 earn about 89 cents for every dollar men earn hourly; for racialised women the figure is closer to 78 cents. Those gaps start forming in the years you are in school now, in which subjects get encouraged and which do not.

A common misconception

"Feminism means women should be above men." It does not. The word for that would be supremacy, and feminism is the argument against exactly that logic. Equality is not a zero-sum transfer: when women entered medicine in large numbers, medicine did not shrink, it grew.

Black feminism is a tradition of thought and organising, built by Black women, that examines racism, sexism, class exploitation and other systems together rather than one at a time, because that is how they are lived. It treats Black women's experience and scholarship as a source of knowledge, not a special case appended to someone else's theory.

First, what "white feminism" means

This is a description of a pattern, not an insult aimed at individual white women. White feminism refers to feminist thinking and campaigning that centres the experiences of white, usually middle-class, women and treats those experiences as universal, while overlooking race, class, disability, immigration status and other systems that shape what being a woman actually costs.

A concrete example: a campaign whose central demand is getting women out of the home and into paid work speaks powerfully to women who were kept out of the workforce. It speaks much less to Black and working-class women, many of whom had been working, often in other women's homes, the entire time. The demand was not wrong. It was partial, and it was presented as if it were everyone's.

What Black feminism adds

It argues that you cannot understand a Black woman's situation by taking "the women's experience" and adding "the Black experience," because neither of those is neutral. The Combahee River Collective put the political conclusion plainly in 1977: if Black women were free, it would mean everyone else had to be free, since their freedom would require dismantling every system at once. That is why this site measures progress from the most excluded person up.

What it looks like at school

Research in Canadian schools has repeatedly found Black girls are read as louder, older and more confrontational than their classmates for identical behaviour, a pattern researchers call adultification. A dress code enforced evenly on paper can still land unevenly in a hallway. A feminism that only counts gender cannot see that; Black feminism is built to.

A common misconception

"Black feminism divides the movement." The reverse claim is the one the evidence supports: it was Black feminists who kept pointing out where feminism's wins were not reaching everyone, and those corrections are why the movement became more accurate. Sojourner Truth was making this argument in 1851, well before there was a name for it.

Intersectionality is a framework for understanding how a person's overlapping identities, and the systems of power attached to them, combine to produce experiences that none of those systems explains on its own. It was named by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989.

Where it came from, and why that matters

Crenshaw was studying real court cases. In one, Black women sued a car manufacturer for discrimination. The court found the company hired women, so it could not be sexist, and hired Black people, so it could not be racist, and dismissed the case. Both findings were true in isolation. The women had been hired for neither set of jobs: the company hired white women for office work and Black men for the factory floor. The discrimination was real and the law could not see it, because the law examined one axis at a time.

That is the whole idea. It is not a statement that some people have harder lives. It is a statement about analysis: examine systems one at a time and you will miss harms that only appear where they meet.

What it looks like at school

A school introduces a rule against head coverings, aimed at hats. A Muslim student who wears hijab is now in breach of a rule that was never written about her. Gender-only analysis misses it, religion-only analysis misses it. The overlap is where it lives.

A common misconception

"Intersectionality is about ranking who is most oppressed." It is not, and Crenshaw has said so directly. It is a tool for seeing structures, not a scoreboard for people. Used as a ranking, it stops doing the job it was built for.

The five things people most often get wrong. Our own interviews found that most misunderstanding traced back to one cause: never having been taught the word in the first place. None of these are stupid questions.

1. "Feminism just means treating girls right"

This one came directly out of our 2025 interviews: both female participants had met it. Being decent to the girls around you is good, and it is not what the word means. Feminism is about rights, pay, safety, representation and law. A person can be perfectly polite and still vote against equal pay legislation. Courtesy is not equality.

2. "Feminists hate men"

The target is a system, not a gender. And the same rigid expectations that feminism criticises are the ones that tell boys not to cry, not to ask for help, and to solve things with aggression. Plenty of feminist writing is about exactly that harm.

3. "We already have equality, so it's finished"

Legal equality and lived equality are different things, and Canada has the gaps documented: the hourly wage gap, one female prime minister in the country's history, and the fact that some women could not vote federally until 1948 or, for many First Nations women, 1960. Recent, not ancient.

4. "It's a Western idea being exported"

Women's movements developed independently in many places and long predate the current vocabulary. Treating one region as the origin point is itself the kind of framing Black and postcolonial feminists have spent decades correcting.

5. "It has nothing to do with me"

If you have a mother, sister, friend or classmate, the wage gap is a household number. If you are a boy who has been told to man up, the script being criticised is one you are being handed too.

Where this actually shows up between 8:45 and 3:15. Our interviews found girls and boys are treated differently at school, including in academics. Here is what that looks like in practice.

Dress codes

Ask who gets pulled out of class for what. When enforcement falls mostly on girls, and the stated reason is that other students will be distracted, the rule has quietly made one group responsible for another group's behaviour. Worth asking your student council to look at the actual numbers.

Who talks in class

The same contribution is often read as confident from one student and aggressive from another. Notice who gets interrupted in seminars and group work. Notice who gets credited when an idea is repeated back five minutes later.

Group projects

Watch who ends up doing the organising, the formatting and the chasing. It is called office housework in workplace research, and it starts well before anyone has an office.

Subject choice

Encouragement is a resource, and it is not handed out evenly. Who gets told they have a knack for physics, and who gets asked whether they are sure.

A common misconception

"If nobody meant it, nothing happened." Intent and effect are separate. Most of what is described above is done by people with no bad intentions at all, which is exactly why noticing patterns matters more than assigning blame.

178 years, walked as a timeline. Four waves, the moments between them, and a quick check at the end of each.

One caution before you start. The "waves" model is a common way of organising this history, and it is also debated by historians. It can make the movement look like it stopped between waves, when organising continued the whole time, which is exactly why the bridge entries below exist. Treat the waves as chapter headings, not as facts about when people were working.
