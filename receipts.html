Receipts

Every number here has to earn its place.

This site makes claims about history and about students. That only means something if you can check it. So here is the method behind the research, a source for every date in the year machine, and the rules the wall runs on.

A note on honesty: this is a student project, not a peer reviewed study. The interviews below involved three people, and the survey is small and still running. Those limits are written into every claim on this page rather than hidden behind a percentage.

Part one

Where the numbers come from.

Two separate pieces of work sit behind this site, and they are easy to mix up. One is the 2025 interview study. The other is the survey still collecting answers right now. They measure different things.

2025 The interview study

Exploring Feminism: Voices of TDSB Students

Presented at the Youth Participatory Action Research showcase run by the TDSB Centre of Excellence for Black Student Achievement, August 12 to 15, 2025.

The question: how does the understanding of feminism among the male and female population influence the experience of young girls, ages 13 to 18, within the TDSB?

The method: qualitative. Three semi structured interviews with TDSB students, chosen by convenience sampling, with follow up questions asked as each conversation went on. Interviews were used instead of a large survey because the goal was depth, not headcount.

What came out of it:

  • Feminism was rarely or never discussed at home. Two of the three interviews said so directly, both from African households.
  • Girls and boys are treated differently at school, including in academics. Raised in two of the three interviews.
  • Every participant said feminism belongs in the curriculum.
  • Most misunderstanding was traced back to never being taught. If you have not been told what the word means, you cannot be expected to back it.
  • Both female participants had met the idea that feminism just means "treating girls right." It does not, and that gap is a large part of why this site exists.

What it cannot tell you: three interviews cannot speak for the TDSB, for Toronto, or for teenagers. Convenience sampling means participants were not randomly selected. Read the findings as a signal worth chasing, not a measurement.

Source: Adusi, E. (2025). Exploring Feminism: Voices of TDSB Students. Presented at the CEBSA YPAR showcase, Toronto District School Board, August 12 to 15, 2025. Later presented at Simon Fraser University in Vancouver.

2026 The pulse survey

This number moves

"Do students find learning about feminism boring?"

The 44% quoted across this site comes from here, not from the 2025 interviews. It is an open survey that any student can answer, and the counter on the Learn page reads the live tally.

As of 27 July 2026: 36 of 82 responses said yes, which is 43.9%. That is where "over 44%" comes from, and it will drift as more people answer. The site only ever receives the yes and no totals, never anybody's individual answer, because the people answering are minors.

What it cannot tell you: anyone can respond, responses are not verified, and people who care about the question are likelier to answer than people who do not. It is a temperature check, not a poll. Treat 44% as roughly four in ten of the students who chose to answer.

Source: We are Feminism pre survey, ongoing since 2026. Live aggregate tally shown on the Learn page.

Part two

Every date on the year machine.

The year machine on Your voice sends you back to 1912 and tells you how old you would have been when each right arrived. Those dates are real. Here is where each one comes from, including the places where the story is more complicated than one line can hold.

1918 The vote, for some

The Women's Franchise Act received royal assent on 24 May 1918, giving women 21 and over the federal vote on the same terms as men. It took effect in 1919, and 1921 was the first federal election women voted in.

The word "most" is carrying real weight. The Act kept the rule that anyone disenfranchised by a province on grounds of race was barred federally too. In British Columbia that covered people of Chinese, Japanese, South Asian and Indigenous origin. Status First Nations women stayed excluded until 1960.

Elections Canada, "The Evolution of the Federal Franchise." elections.ca

1929 The Persons Case

On 18 October 1929 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London ruled in Edwards v Canada that "qualified persons" in the British North America Act included women, making them eligible for appointment to the Senate. It overturned a 1928 Supreme Court of Canada decision. Cairine Wilson became the first woman senator in February 1930.

Where the popular version overshoots: the ruling was about Senate eligibility, not a declaration that women had been non persons in law generally. The site's line is written to say the narrower, true thing.

Senate of Canada, "Why the Persons Case Matters." sencanada.ca

1947
1948 Race out of the franchise

The Dominion Elections Act of 1920 disqualified federally anyone a province had disenfranchised by race, so the federal date moved when the provincial one did.

1947: British Columbia removed the disqualification for Chinese and South Asian residents on 3 April 1947, which restored their federal vote that year. 1948: Parliament deleted the race based disqualification from the federal Act itself, which reached Japanese Canadians, stripped of the vote in 1944 after the forced removal from BC. The first ballots were cast in the June 1949 election. Won Alexander Cumyow, the first Chinese Canadian born in Canada, voted for the first time in 1949 at the age of 88.

Elections Canada, "A History of the Vote in Canada," chapter 3. elections.ca

1960 The vote without the price

On 10 March 1960 the House of Commons voted to give First Nations people the federal vote without enfranchisement, the process that stripped Indian status in exchange. Before this, registered Indians living on reserve were specifically disqualified. Inuit were entitled to vote from 1950, though ballot boxes did not reach the eastern Arctic until 1962.

Elections Canada, "A History of the Vote in Canada," chapter 3. elections.ca

1964 Quebec, Bill 16

An Act respecting the legal capacity of married women was introduced by Marie-Claire Kirkland-Casgrain, Quebec's first woman MNA, and came into force on 1 July 1964. It gave married women full legal capacity, let them sign contracts and manage property without a husband's authorization, and abolished a wife's legal duty of obedience. Kirkland-Casgrain had herself needed her husband to sign the lease on her Quebec City apartment.

Not the finish line: Bill 16 kept the husband as legal head of the family. That was not removed until 1980.

Bibliothèque de l'Assemblée nationale du Québec, "1964 - Bill 16." bibliotheque.assnat.qc.ca

1965 The last segregated schools

Ontario's last segregated Black school, S.S. No. 11 in Colchester South Township near Harrow, closed in 1965. It had been open since 1825. Leonard Braithwaite, Ontario's first Black MPP, attacked the 1850 segregation provision in his maiden speech in February 1964, and the Separate Schools Act was amended a month later to strike every reference to segregated schools. The closure was pushed by local Black parents and the South Essex Coloured Citizens' Association, not by the repeal alone.

The Nova Scotia half needs care: the last Black school there, in Lincolnville, Guysborough County, stayed open until 1983 and is widely described as the last in Canada. But Nova Scotia ended segregation in law in 1954, so that school was segregated in practice, not by statute. An earlier version of this site called it "legally segregated." That was wrong and has been corrected.

Ontario College of Teachers, Professionally Speaking, "Ontario's Last Segregated School." professionallyspeaking.oct.ca

1969 Birth control

The Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1968-69 received royal assent on 27 June 1969 and took contraceptives out of the Criminal Code's provisions on offences tending to corrupt morals. The ban, in place since the 1892 Criminal Code, was on selling, advertising and distributing contraception rather than on using it, which is why the site's line is worded that way.

The Canadian Encyclopedia, "History of Birth Control in Canada." thecanadianencyclopedia.ca

1971 Maternity benefits

The Unemployment Insurance Act of 1971 introduced 15 weeks of maternity benefits at 66% of previous earnings, for mothers with at least 20 weeks of insurable earnings.

This one was corrected: the site used to say federal law protected your job on maternity leave from 1971. That was wrong twice over. 1971 was about money, not job protection, and the federal job protection that did exist covered only federally regulated workplaces such as banks, airlines and telecom, which is a small share of workers. For everyone else, job protection came from provincial employment standards on a patchwork of different dates. British Columbia got there first, making it unlawful to dismiss a woman for a maternity absence back in 1921.

Statistics Canada, "Parental leave, 1997 to 2022," Quality of Employment in Canada. statcan.gc.ca

1983 Sexual assault law

Bill C-127 came into force on 4 January 1983. It replaced the offences of rape, attempted rape and indecent assault with three sexual assault offences, and removed the spousal immunity, so a husband could be charged with sexually assaulting his wife. Before this, the offence of rape was defined as intercourse with a woman "who is not his wife," so marriage was not so much a defence as an exclusion written into the law itself.

The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Sexual Assault." thecanadianencyclopedia.ca

1985 Charter equality and Bill C-31

Section 15 of the Charter came into force on 17 April 1985, three years after the rest of the Charter, a delay written in deliberately to give governments time to fix discriminatory laws. Bill C-31 amended the Indian Act that June, retroactive to 17 April so the Act would comply with section 15. It repealed the provision under which a status woman lost her status by marrying a man without status, while a status man did not. More than 100,000 people gained or regained status.

Why the site says "begin to win it back": C-31 was incomplete and left second generation discrimination in place, which took Bill C-3 in 2011 and Bill S-3 in 2017 and 2019 to keep addressing.

The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Bill C-31." thecanadianencyclopedia.ca · Department of Justice Canada, Charterpedia, section 15. justice.gc.ca

1993 The only one

Kim Campbell was sworn in as Prime Minister on 25 June 1993 and left office on 4 November 1993. She remains the only woman to have held the office.

The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Kim Campbell." thecanadianencyclopedia.ca

The line at 68

Read this one carefully

Why milestones past 68 are dimmed

The year machine ghosts every right that arrived after the visitor would have turned 68. That number is not life expectancy at birth. Life expectancy at birth for Canadian women of that era was far lower, around 60 in 1920 to 1922 and lower still in the 1890s, because roughly one baby in ten died before their first birthday and that pulls the average down hard.

The visitor in the year machine is already 16 and alive, so the honest statistic is the conditional one: how long could a woman expect to live given that she survived childhood. Statistics Canada's historical tables put female life expectancy at age 20 at 47.8 further years in 1881, which lands at about 68, and 49.2 further years in 1921, about 69.

Three limits worth knowing. The table measures from age 20, not 16, and a 16 year old in 1912 still faced tuberculosis, childbirth and the 1918 influenza pandemic before getting there. The 1881 figure covers only four provinces. And these are period life tables, which freeze one year's death rates and project them forward, so real women who reached 20 often outlived 68 as mortality fell through the century. That is why the site says a milestone "most likely" arrived after she was gone, rather than claiming she never saw it.

Statistics Canada, Historical Statistics of Canada, Table B65-74, "Life expectancy by sex, at selected ages, Canada, census years, 1871 to 1971." statcan.gc.ca · Statistics Canada, "Life expectancy, 1920-1922 to 2009-2011." statcan.gc.ca

2026 The gap that is still open

This number moves

Statistics Canada, reporting 2025 average hourly wages from the Labour Force Survey: women aged 25 to 54 earn 89 cents for every dollar earned by men, up from 81 cents in 1997. For women 15 and over it is 88 cents.

Split by population group, measured against men who are neither Indigenous nor racialized: racialized women earn 78 cents, Indigenous women 79 cents, and non-Indigenous non-racialized women 88 cents.

Why Black women are not named in that same sentence. The best figure for Black women specifically comes from a different study on a different basis: measured over a full year rather than by the hour, Black women's employment earnings were 58.5% of those of white Canadian born men. Annual measures produce much larger gaps than hourly ones because they also capture hours worked. Putting 89 cents and 58.5% in one breath would imply Black women's hourly pay is 58 cents, which is not what either source says. An earlier version of the site did blur these together. It now uses only the numbers that share a basis.

Statistics Canada, "The gender wage gap persists," published 5 March 2026. statcan.gc.ca · Bonikowska, A. (2024). "Gender earnings ratio differences among population groups in Canada," Statistics Canada. statcan.gc.ca

Part three

House rules for the wall.

The wall is anonymous and it is mostly read by teenagers. That combination only works if everyone holds the same line. Five rules, and they are not complicated.

The rules

  1. Leave names out of it.

    Not yours, not your school's, not the name of anyone your story is about. No handles, no phone numbers, no email addresses. You can tell the entire truth without handing over anyone's identity, and that includes the identity of someone who hurt you.

  2. This wall lifts people up.

    Support, questions, and hard honest stories all belong here. Cruelty, harassment, and posts that exist to make someone else feel small do not.

  3. Never out anyone.

    Someone's race, religion, disability, sexuality, gender identity, or immigration status is theirs to share. Sharing it for them can put a person in real danger.

  4. Care with the heavy stuff.

    You can say that you are struggling. What is not allowed is detail that teaches another reader how to hurt themselves. If you are in it right now, the wall is not fast enough to help you. The numbers below are.

  5. Respond like it is someone you love.

    Every reply here goes to a real person who worked up the nerve to post. If you cannot be gentle, close the tab. "I read this and I believe you" is enough.

What happens if a post breaks these. Right now the wall is still local, so posts stay on the device that wrote them and nobody else can see them. Once it opens up properly, posts will be reviewed before they appear, anything breaking these rules will be removed, and posts describing someone in danger will be passed to people who can actually help. That is the trade for keeping it anonymous.
