References and sources

Every claim, and where to go and check it.

A site that tells you feminism is worth learning has to be checkable. So here is the whole list: every date, number and definition on this site, next to the place it came from. Most of these are free to read and none of them need an account.

The words

The three lenses on the Learn page are not our coinages. Each one comes from a specific writer, in a specific year, and reading them first-hand is a better hour than anything we could write about them.

  1. 1977

    Black feminism defines the everyday experiences of Black women and girls in their own words, and measures freedom from the most excluded person up.

    The Combahee River Collective Statement, April 1977. Reprinted in Barbara Smith, ed., Home Girls: A Black Feminist Anthology (Kitchen Table: Women of Color Press, 1983). Full text at BlackPast (opens in a new tab).

  2. 1989

    Intersectionality is the knot that ties everything together: race, gender and class, all at once, under one movement.

    Kimberlé Crenshaw, "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex," University of Chicago Legal Forum 1989, no. 1, article 8. Free full text at Chicago Unbound (opens in a new tab).

  3. 1981

    bell hooks is a pen name, taken from her great-grandmother Bell Blair Hooks and kept lowercase on purpose. The title of Ain't I a Woman is the separate borrowing, from Sojourner Truth.

    National Women's History Museum, "bell hooks" (opens in a new tab).

The history

The timeline runs to fifteen events across 178 years. Every one of them is below, in the order you meet it, with the reference we wrote it from. Where a Canadian date is contested or staged (the vote arrived at different times for different women), we have linked the source that says so plainly rather than the one that rounds it off.

  1. 1851

    Sojourner Truth speaks at a women's convention in Akron, Ohio, and names what the movement was leaving out.

    U.S. National Park Service, Women's Rights National Historical Park (opens in a new tab). Every surviving transcript of the speech is second-hand and they differ from each other; the best-known "Ar'n't I a woman?" wording was published twelve years later.

  2. 1916

    Manitoba becomes the first Canadian province where women win the vote, after years of petitions and a satirical Mock Parliament.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Women's Suffrage in Canada" (opens in a new tab).

  3. 1918

    Women win the federal vote, unless they were of Asian descent, or First Nations, or Inuit. Many waited until 1948, or 1960.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Right to Vote in Canada" (opens in a new tab), which carries the staged dates for each excluded group.

  4. 1929

    The Persons Case: the Famous Five take "are women persons?" to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, and win.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Persons Case" (opens in a new tab).

  5. 1946

    Viola Desmond refuses to leave the whites-only floor of a New Glasgow theatre and is arrested over one cent of tax. She is on the $10 bill now.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Viola Desmond" (opens in a new tab).

  6. 1960

    The pill arrives in Canada, where it could only be prescribed "for cycle regulation" until 1969.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Birth Control in Canada" (opens in a new tab).

  7. 1967

    The Royal Commission on the Status of Women is launched, and comes back with 167 recommendations.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Royal Commission on the Status of Women in Canada" (opens in a new tab).

  8. 1977

    Black feminists in Boston write the statement that reframes everything: if Black women were free, everybody would have to be free.

    The Combahee River Collective Statement, as above.

  9. 1981

    bell hooks publishes Ain't I a Woman: Black Women and Feminism and puts Black women back at the centre of the story.

    South End Press, 1981. National Women's History Museum, "bell hooks" (opens in a new tab).

  10. 1989

    Kimberlé Crenshaw coins one word, intersectionality, for what Sojourner, Viola and Combahee had been living all along.

    Crenshaw 1989, as above.

  11. 1991

    Riot grrrl zines: photocopied, stapled, passed hand to hand. Girls making their own media instead of waiting to be covered fairly.

    The Riot Grrrl Collection, Fales Library and Special Collections, New York University (opens in a new tab), which holds the zines themselves.

  12. 1995

    Beijing: 189 countries sign the biggest agreement on gender equality the world has made.

    UN Women, World Conferences on Women (opens in a new tab). The agreement itself is the Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action (opens in a new tab).

  13. 2006

    Tarana Burke starts "me too" as a phrase for survivors to find each other, eleven years before the hashtag.

    me too. Movement, "History & Inception" (opens in a new tab).

  14. 2012

    Idle No More: four women in Saskatchewan, three First Nations and one settler ally, spark an Indigenous rights movement.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Idle No More" (opens in a new tab).

  15. 2013

    #BlackLivesMatter is founded by three Black women: Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors and Opal Tometi.

    The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Black Lives Matter-Canada" (opens in a new tab), which names all three and the 2013 founding.

  16. 2017

    #MeToo goes viral and reaches more than 85 countries in days.

    me too. Movement, "History & Inception" (opens in a new tab).

Our own research

Two numbers on this site are ours, and they come from two different things. Neither is a national study, and neither is presented as one.

  1. 2025

    "I asked students how feminism should be taught." The quotes about feminism sounding like homework, and about nobody saying anything at school, come from here.

    A youth-led research project run with the Toronto District School Board in 2025: a small number of sit-down interviews with students, not a survey. It is the reason this site exists, not evidence of what all students think.

  2. Live

    "44% say learning about it is boring."

    The anonymous pulse survey linked from the Learn page and the QR code on the poster. It is still open and the number on the page is live, so it moves: whatever it reads when you load it is the current tally, not a figure from a report. Only the Yes and No totals are ever collected, and no other answer, and nothing that identifies anyone. Take it yourself (opens in a new tab).
